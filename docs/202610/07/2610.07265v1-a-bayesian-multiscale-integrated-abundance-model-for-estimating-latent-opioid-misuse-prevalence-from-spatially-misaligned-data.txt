Abstract 

Estimating small area prevalence of opioid misuse is critical for targeting public health interventions, yet direct measures are unavailable and related surveillance data are often reported on misaligned areal units. We propose a Bayesian Multiscale Integrated Abundance (MIA) model for estimating latent opioid misuse prevalence by jointly analyzing multiple indirect surveillance indicators observed on different geographic supports. Latent prevalence at the atomic level is modeled using a Fisher noncentral hypergeometric distribution, which preserves county-level prevalence totals while respecting local population constraints. To enable scalable inference, we develop a two-stage compositional Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm that combines customized sampling strategies and parallel computing. Simulation studies show the proposed approach reduces bias and root mean squared error relative to common downscaling methods. We apply the model to Ohio data from 2010-2023, integrating state-level survey estimates, county-level counts of opioid overdose deaths and treatment admissions, and ZIP code tabulation area-level counts of emergency medical services naloxone administrations. Results reveal substantial within-county heterogeneity and identify localized areas of elevated opioid misuse prevalence that would be missed by county-level analyses.

# 1 Introduction 

The opioid epidemic remains a major public health crisis in the United States. Approxi-mately 76% of the 105,000 drug overdose deaths nationwide in 2023 involved opioids. While the total number of overdose deaths involving an opioid declined 4% from 2022 to 2023, the number is still nearly ten times higher than it was in 1999 (CDC, 2025). The impact of the opioid epidemic has been especially severe in the state of Ohio. In 2017, Ohio had the second highest age-adjusted opioid overdose death rate at 39.2 deaths per 100,000, and it reached a peak age-adjusted rate of 40.1 in 2021 before beginning to decline. In 2024, the age-adjusted opioid overdose death rate in Ohio had dropped to 20.1 deaths per 100,000 population. Despite being at its lowest level since 2014, that number remains over 13 times higher than the corresponding rate in 1999 (KFF, 2026). Effective public health responses to the opioid epidemic require information on where opioid misuse is occurring and how it varies across communities. While surveillance data such as opioid overdose death counts provide proxy information for opioid misuse prevalence 2(OMP), any surveillance indicator only provides partial information about the underlying population at risk since not all people who misuse opioids (PWMO) experience an observ-able outcome. Ideally, estimates of OMP would be used to evaluate policy, guide resource allocation, identify health service gaps, and highlight emerging hotspots. However, OMP is not observed directly at finer spatial scales. The National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) generates estimates of OMP, but these are at the state and multi-year level (SAMHSA and Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, 2015). Thus, small area estimates of OMP must be inferred from existing indirect data sources such as overdose death counts, counts of treatment admissions, and counts of naloxone administrations. Indirect estimation is a common strategy for estimating hidden or hard-to-reach pop-ulation sizes and has been applied to estimate OMP and opioid use disorder. Capture-recapture (Jones et al., 2016; Barocas et al., 2018; Min et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a), benchmark-multiplier (Keyes et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024b), and multi-parameter estimation of prevalence (Markoulidakis et al., 2024, 2026) models have all been used to leverage multiple sources of data to estimate population prevalence. Each require at least some data that are individually-linked across data sources or estimates de-rived from surveys and studies with individual-level data. However, government agencies in many jurisdictions only release data in aggregate form to protect privacy and comply with legal obligations. These restrictions do not allow for use of methods based on individual-level identification and linkage and require alternative approaches based exclusively on aggregate data sources. Given privacy and aggregation restrictions, methods have been developed that rely exclusively on aggregate data. One such method uses a top-down approach with a multi-nomial distribution to infer county-level counts of opioid use disorder across the United States using county-level covariates to downscale state-level counts (Peterson et al., 2025). Another alternative is the Bayesian Integrated Abundance (BIA) model, which was devel-3oped by Hepler et al. (2023) to infer latent county-level OMP by jointly modeling multi-ple county-level surveillance outcomes. By modeling multiple aggregate outcomes jointly with state-level NSDUH survey estimates of OMP, the BIA overcomes identifiability issues known to occur with abundance models. They applied the BIA to data from Ohio on opi-oid overdose deaths and treatment admissions to infer yearly county-level OMP. Another advantage of the BIA is it permits quantification of outcome-specific rates among PWMO, which is often more meaningful than outcome rates per population. The BIA has since been applied to other states including New York (Santaella-Tenorio et al., 2024) and North Carolina (Kline et al., 2025), and it has been used to identify counties where there are discrepancies between OMP and buprenorphine prescribing (McKnight et al., 2023). The approaches described above generate estimates at the county-level. Many local health departments are organized at the county level so county-level OMP estimates are particularly valuable for state health officials tasked with allocating resources. However, counties are large, heterogeneous regions, and county-level estimates obscure within-county variability and can thus fail to identify higher resolution geographical regions with elevated OMP, which is critical information for local health departments. As one example, McMaster et al. (2025) examined drug-related mortality at the Census tract level within Cuyahoga County, Ohio. They found that while the county average drug-related mortality rate is near the statewide average, there are Census tracts within the county whose rates are substantially higher. The authors emphasize the need for more granular geographic analysis to facilitate targeted public health interventions. While this analysis was focused on drug-related mortality, we expect there is similarly large within-county variation in OMP. While most surveillance data on opioid-related indicators are available at the county level, there are instances where data on a subset of indicators are available at a finer spatial resolution such as Census tracts or ZIP codes. A major challenge when integrating such data sources is that the spatial units on which they are reported are often misaligned and 4sometimes even non-nested, creating an areal spatial change-of-support (COS) problem. Gotway and Young (2002) provide a broad overview to combining misaligned data. Bradley et al. (2016) and Bradley et al. (2015) used a Bayesian COS “bottom up” strategy to model misaligned data assuming observed data on areal units were aggregates of a spatial point process. While this has the advantage of allowing the user to integrate to any desired areal spatial support, this was designed for settings where one is modeling misaligned data on the same quantity of interest. “Top-down” approaches have commonly been used for COS with areal count data. These use a hierarchical Poisson regression model to model a latent spatial process on the finest areal spatial resolution that is linked to the outcomes through aggregation to the coarser geographical scales. This framework has been used to study the relationship between a response variable and misaligned explanatory variables and to downscale the coarse response variable to a finer spatial resolution. See, for example, Mugglin and Carlin (1998); Mugglin et al. (1999, 2000) and Peterson et al. (2025). Although there is a deep literature on spatial COS, the emphasis is on downscaling an observed variable. In our setting, we do not observe county- or sub-county-level OMP. Instead, methods to indirectly infer sub-county latent OMP that formally integrate multiple sources of information across spatially misaligned areal units are needed. In this paper, we propose the multiscale integrated abundance (MIA) model that ex-tends the Bayesian integrated abundance framework of Hepler et al. (2023). The proposed model jointly analyzes surveillance indicators observed on multiple geographic supports and estimates OMP at both county and sub-county resolutions. By representing all source geographies through a common set of atomic spatial units formed by their intersections, the model accommodates spatially misaligned data while preserving consistency across scales. The resulting framework allows public health agencies to estimate latent OMP at a much finer geographic resolution than is possible using county-level surveillance data alone. There are two main contributions in this paper. The first is the development of the MIA 5model to provide multiscale estimates that can be used to address the needs of both state and local public health officials. A novel aspect of the MIA is the integration of the Fisher Noncentral Hypergeometric (FNCH) distribution as a model for the latent atomic-level process to enforce sub-county constraints related to small area population. This model also provides estimates of OMP at all geographical levels that have standard epidemio-logical interpretations that can be readily used by health department staff and help to ease integration into practice. The second contribution is the development of a two-stage Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach that novelly combines parallel computing with strategic sampling algorithms to efficiently satisfy model constraints while remain-ing scalable and generalizable. While our work is motivated by the need for estimates of OMP in Ohio, the MIA model can more generally be applied to prevalence estimation of hard-to-reach populations where only data on aggregate indirect indicators are available. Section 2 introduces the data from Ohio that motivated our methodological develop-ment. The MIA model and two-stage MCMC approach are described in Section 3. In Sec-tion 4, we present simulation results that compare the MIA model’s sub-county estimates to estimates that would be obtained through commonly used downscaling approaches, and in Section 5, we apply the MIA to estimate yearly, sub-county OMP in Ohio. 

# 2 Data and Motivating Application 

Our proposed model integrates multiple data sources at varying spatial and temporal scales, with the goal of performing multiscale inference on OMP in Ohio. Below, we describe the different data sources used, broken down by spatial resolution. 62.1 State-Level Data 

The only direct data source available on OMP comes from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) (SAMHSA, Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, 2003). Prior to 2015, this survey provided estimates of past-year nonmedical opioid use, and post-2015 it estimated past-year opioid misuse. Despite the change in survey language, we treat these measures as comparable over time. The NSDUH reports multi-year averaged estimates of statewide prevalence for Ohio, along with associated standard errors. We use estimates spanning 2007–2022 to inform the statewide prevalence trajectory. The survey estimates and standard errors are shown in Supplemental Material (SM) Table 1. 

## 2.2 County-Level Data 

The county-level data included is the same as in Hepler et al. (2023), but updated to include more recent years. More specifically, for each of Ohio’s 88 counties and each year from 2010 - 2023, we use two surveillance indicators: opioid overdose death counts and counts of treatment admissions. Death counts come from the Ohio Public Data Warehouse and include yearly county-level counts of deaths where the death certificate mentions poisoning from any opioid under the ICD-10 codes T40.0-T40.4 and T40.6. Deaths are indexed by the county of residence of the decedent. The treatment admissions counts represent unique patients and come from the Ohio Department of Behavioral Health through a data use agreement that includes counts of admissions to residential, outpatient, or intensive outpatient treatment for opioid use disorder. Treatment counts under 50 are suppressed. County-level population data is from the National Center for Health Statistics and obtained through the Ohio Public Health Data Warehouse (Ohio Public Health Data Warehouse, 2020). Maps of the county-level indicators are shown in SM Figures 1 and 2. Our county-level model includes other county-level variables as covariates. Sociodemo-7graphic variables come from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates. The ACS variables included are the poverty rate, unemployment rate, proportion with at least a high school degree, and the proportion on food stamps. We also include data on opioid pre-scribing rates, which is publicly available from the Ohio Automated RX Reporting System. Additionally, we compile county-level indicators of being designated a health professional shortage area (HPSA) or a medically underserved (MU) area from the Health Resources and Services Administration, a high impact drug trafficking area (HIDTA) from the Drug Enforcement Agency, a metropolitan statistical area (MSA) from the United States Census Bureau, and an indicator for having an interstate highway intersect the county. 

## 2.3 ZCTA-Level Data 

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist used to reverse overdose. For 2017 to 2023, we have indicator data on emergency medical services (EMS)-administered naloxone counts aggre-gated to the ZIP-code level. These data were obtained from the Ohio Department of Public Safety Division of Emergency Medical Services, through the EMS Incident Report-ing System (Ohio Emergency Medical Services, 2026). Counts reflect EMS administrations recorded by EMS staff regardless of the final cause of the incident and do not reflect unique patients. We process all ZIP-code level data to the ZIP-code tabulation area (ZCTA) level because U.S. Census data sources provide demographic information at the ZCTA-level. This processing is done using a crosswalk obtained from the Missouri Census Data Center. For a detailed description, see Murphy et al. (2025). We also removed remaining ZCTA that corresponded to single delivery-point address. The final dataset includes 1063 ZCTAs. The ZCTA-level rates of EMS administered naloxone are shown in SM Figures 3-5. ZCTA-level covariates are included to capture spatial variation in EMS-administered naloxone rates. We include the proportion of each ZCTA who are unemployed and the proportion with at least a Bachelor’s degree from the ACS 2018 - 2022 5-year estimates. 8We construct indicators of urbanicity based on the rural-urban commuting area (RUCA) codes. We define ZCTAs with RUCA codes between 1-3 to be ‘metropolitan’ and ZCTAs with RUCA codes between 4-6 to be ‘micropolitan,’ and we include two indicator covariates for each designation with codes between 7-10 as a ‘rural’ reference group. 

## 2.4 Atom-Level Data 

Throughout, we will follow the terminology of Mugglin et al. (1999), Bradley et al. (2016), and Trevisani and Gelfand (2013) and define the source zones to be the areal units where count data are available, and the target zones as the area where we wish to make inference. Atoms are the spatial units constructed by intersecting our source geographies. In our case, counties and ZCTAs serve as the source support and atoms, which are the intersection of counties and ZCTAs, are the target support. Note that while counties and ZCTAs are non-nested, atoms are nested both within counties and ZCTAs. This construction ensures all modeled quantities can be expressed as sums over a common set of atomic units, permitting multiscale inference at both the target and source levels of geography. Our study consists of 1670 atoms across Ohio. Figure 1 illustrates source and target geographies in our application zoomed into central Ohio. We obtain atom-level population counts using the 2022 Geographic Correspondence Engine from the Missouri Census Data Center (Missouri Census Data Center, 2022). This provided county-level population counts from the 2020 Census and the proportion of the county that resided within each atom formed by intersecting counties and ZCTAs. We also included the proportion of the atom population that is unemployed and the proportion with at least a bachelor’s degree. These atom-level covariates were constructed by assuming the ZCTA-level rates from the 2018-2022 5-year ACS estimates were constant for all atoms within the ZCTA. Maps of the atom-level covariates are included in SM Figures 6 and 7. 9Figure 1: The left map shows the two source zones - Central Ohio counties (black lines) and the ZCTA that intersect them (gray lines). Without loss of generality and for ease of exposition, we describe the model assuming count-valued aggregate data on counties and ZCTAs; however, the framework can be applied for any type of areal spatial units (e.g., Census tracts, cities, etc) and straightforwardly extended to more than two types of geographical units.

# 5 Application 

We fit the proposed MIA model to the Ohio data described in Section 2. Briefly, we jointly model state-level survey estimates on OMP, county-level counts of opioid overdose deaths and treatment admissions, and ZCTA-level counts of EMS-administered naloxone (post-processed from ZIP-code level data as previously described). We fit the model using the two-stage compositional MCMC approach described in Section 3.3. For the coarser county-level component of the model, we include covariates, X(k)

For the treatment rate, we include indicators of the county being a health professional shortage area and a medically underserved area. To model the county-level OMP ratio, λit , we include standardized covariates on the proportion of the county in poverty, unemployed, with at least a high school degree, and on food stamps, as well as the rate of prescription opioids dispensed. # 6 Discussion 

In this paper, we developed the multiscale integrated abundance model (MIA), which builds on the BIA of Hepler et al. (2023) to estimate OMP at the sub-county level while remaining simultaneously interpretable at the county level. The MIA is designed so that each layer of the model is epidemiologically interpretable as areal regression models for disease map-ping. Latent sub-county OMP estimates are generated using a novel approach based on the FNCH which incorporates constraints requiring sub-county atom-level counts within a county to sum to the county total and to not exceed the atom total population. To make the model fitting computationally feasible, we developed a two-stage compositional MCMC algorithm with an approximate MH update to overcome the computational intractability of the normalizing constant associated with the FNCH. A simulation study verified that the MIA could recover atom-level prevalence estimates and outperformed naive competing approaches. We applied the MIA to data from Ohio to estimate county- and sub-county-level OMP to further characterize heterogeneity and provide estimates that can improve 28 local targeting of public health services and interventions. Estimates from the MIA make significant contributions to the epidemiological un-derstanding and public health response to the opioid epidemic. Previously, statewide, population-based estimates were primarily limited to the county level. More local analysis were typically concentrated within specific cities or counties (Wesson et al., 2018; McNeely et al., 2012). With the MIA, we are now able to provide county and sub-county estimates of OMP jointly for all counties in the state. Because of constraints on sub-county estimates, we ensure that estimates within the model are internally consistent so one can draw the same conclusions regardless of which spatial unit is being considered. This enables MIA estimates to be used by state officials to help guide resources to counties and to be used by counties to help optimize the response within their jurisdiction. In addition, the model enables the incorporation of covariates and retains standard epidemiological interpretations of parameters which facilitates exploration of other social environmental questions of in-terest and eases the integration of this work into practice. The general MIA framework can also be applied to other challenging to measure health outcomes to integrate multiple sources of data to generate small area estimates. The MIA modeling framework opens up a range of potential future research. The avail-ability of sub-county-level OMP estimates enable a more granular examination of questions related to access and gaps in the availability of health services. Prior to the MIA, questions were limited to the county-level which served as crude approximations to local accessibility of services. Future methodological research could continue to explore ways to estimate OMP on finer geographic scales, should such indicator data be available. Extensions could include connections to and adaptations of the work of Bradley et al. (2016) which would enable estimation at any geographical unit of choice. This would be particularly advanta-geous as different health departments and service organizations may have different priorities and require different spatial units of analysis. However, a unified framework across units 29 of analysis would be highly desirable so that resulting inferences and actions are consistent across agencies and not dependent on the choice of the unit of analysis. Like any model for estimating a hidden population, potential departures from assump-tions represent limitations. Estimates of OMP counts depend on the NSDUH survey re-flecting a reasonable scale for OMP. However, prevalence ratios are robust to the quality of the NSDUH survey data. We also assume that all individuals counted in each indica-tor are members of the population of interest, PWMO, and like any surveillance data, a chance of misclassification (i.e., false positives) may exist. It is also unlikely that all EMS naloxone administrations occur in the home ZIP code of the patient, although we believe this still serves as a reasonable sub-county indicator. Such spatial misattribution is a topic for future research. Spatial resolution of the estimates are limited by the availability of finer scale indicators; however, the 2-stage computing approach enables the incorporation of new indicators as they become available. Finally, we had to use an approximate MH up-date for θA parameters for computational feasibility; however, the approximation is likely reasonable as OMP is relatively low. In conclusion, the MIA integrates data from multiple sources at multiple levels of aggre-gation of spatially misaligned units to produce multiscale estimates of OMP while quan-tifying their associated uncertainty. The multiscale estimates enable decision-makers at the state and local levels of the public health system to have estimates that are relevant to their jurisdictions that are jointly estimated and consistent across levels of aggregation. Estimates from the MIA are a major advancement in understanding local epidemiology of opioid misuse and inform the targeted deployment of public health services and interven-tions to prevent morbidity and mortality associated with opioid misuse. 30 References
